## Show Notes

# Real Contradictions
Right at the beginning of Chapter 3.2 The Means of Circulation, Marx says something rather interesting.

"We saw earlier that the exchange process of commodities implies contradictory and mutually exclusive conditions. As commodities develop, these contradictions don’t disappear, but a form arises in which they can move. Real contradictions are generally resolved in this way. We express a contradiction, for example, when we say that one body is constantly both falling toward another body and falling away from it. The ellipse is one of the forms of motion through which this contradiction is just as much realized as resolved." (p. 78) <span title="Man sah, daß der Austauschprozeß der Waren widersprechende und einander ausschließende Beziehungen einschließt. Die Entwicklung der Ware hebt diese Widersprüche nicht auf, schafft aber die Form, worin sie sich bewegen können. Dies ist überhaupt die Methode, wodurch sich wirkliche Widersprüche lösen. Es ist z.B. ein Widerspruch, daß ein Körper beständig in einen andren fällt und ebenso beständig von ihm wegflieht. Now, I've been saying throughout the show that something like _dimensionality_ plays a big role in the theory that Marx develops. My go to not-from-Marx example is that a circle is a form of appearance of a cylinder/can (the one that you get when you look at it from the top). That's a two-dimensional "appearance" of a three-dimensional object - and appearances like that are something like partial truths (but which one must not mistake for the whole truth). Etc. Marx himself gives plenty of examples like that, both geometric and non-geometric. In chapter 1.4, for example he says that vision is about [at the very least, I might carefully add] a relation between the eye and some external object that we're seeing, but we experience it merely as the form of the thing perceived. A dimensionality reduction: a relation, something with two participants, appears as a property, something with just one - or, a two-dimensional thing appears as merely one of its dimensions, and hence a one-dimensional thing.

So what's going on with the ellipse? Let me walk you through the geometry and contradiction of it, as I understand it.
What we have here is, I believe, simply an _increase_ in dimensionality! Marx calls it a contradiction "that one body is constantly both falling toward another body and falling away from it". Sounds true enough! So, what the hell is he talking about with that ellipse? Well, we could treat _distance_ not as a one-dimensional vector (something simply measured in, say, meters) -- and then distance can indeed not increase and decrease at the same time. But when we treat distance as a thing with two components, in a two-dimensional system -- then the two components get a certain amount of "relative independence". One may increase, while the other one decreases. 
Let's take a look: Our green dot moves along an elipsis. When we break down distance into the two components of the x-axis and the y-axis, they look like this. Play around with it:

If you look carefully (or read the text in the bottom right), an elliptical path is a form in which, whenever x increases, y decreases - and vice versa. That is to say, whenever one dimension is "falling toward another body", the other is "falling away from it". This is not an accidental property of an ellipse - it's a necessary, definitional, characteristic property of ellipses. And hence, the particular kind of "unity" of X and Y that we call an ellipse is a "forms of motion through which this contradiction is just as much **realized as resolved**."

And what are we talking about in this particular chapter? We have already been talking about the fact that commodities have an internal contradiction -- that between Use Value and Value (a contradiction between two different ways of relating to an object). Now we are talking about the fact that these two things start to have their own, interrelated but distinct forms of movement! An "**inner unity [that] moves via external oppositions**"!

Towards the end of the section (p. 87), Marx says this:

"No one can sell unless someone else buys. But no one needs to buy immediately just because he’s sold something else. Circulation explodes the temporal, spatial, and individual limits that go with the direct exchange of products, and it does so by breaking up the immediate identity involved in direct exchange—disposing of one’s own labor product and acquiring someone else’s. What was once that immediate identity now becomes the opposition between selling and buying. These processes take place as mutually independent opposites but form an inner unity, which means that this inner unity moves via external oppositions. If things that complete each other internally, and thus aren’t independent, become externally  independent past a certain point, then their unity will make itself felt with great force, by way of ...  *a crisis*.
The commodity contains an inherent opposition: use-value versus value"

I think this is exactly what the elipse example is supposed to clarify.
